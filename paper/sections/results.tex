Every number in this section traces to an archived run record, and
Appendix~\ref{app:prov} names the record behind each table and figure. Unless
otherwise stated, values are single runs at a fixed seed; no confidence
intervals are reported for them because none is statistically defensible at
one run per configuration. The order sweep, the station-selection comparison
and the other single-factor \emph{ablations} in this section are a
single-airfoil matrix on NACA 2412 plus two additional single-airfoil runs on
NACA 0012 and 4415. These are deliberately narrow comparisons designed to
isolate one factor at a time, not panel studies, and convergence-fraction
language is avoided in those subsections for that reason.
Sections~\ref{sec:h1-panel} and~\ref{sec:h1-uiuc-panel} are the exceptions:
they report the two pre-registered panels, run subsequently at the
configuration these ablations identify, and their interval estimates are
stated there on their own terms.

\paragraph{Station-addressing provenance.} The ablation matrix in this section
was run with target stations addressed by panel-node index, which was the
implementation at the time. Addressing was subsequently changed to surface and
chordwise fraction with interpolation between the two bracketing nodes,
because a node index stops denoting the same physical location once the
geometry moves, which is precisely what an inverse solve does. The change
moves the reference-configuration figures quoted in
Section~\ref{sec:falsifiable} from $1.08\x10^{-11}$ in four iterations to
$2.75\x10^{-11}$ in six, both far inside the criterion, and leaves the
QR-selected submap conditioning unchanged at $90.3$. Every run in this section
shares the earlier addressing, so the within-matrix comparisons the ablations
rest on are unaffected; what must not be done is to read a single number from
this section alongside a single number from Section~\ref{sec:falsifiable} as
though they came from one run. Where that distinction matters it is stated at
the point of use.

\subsection{Station-selection identifiability}
\label{sec:ablations}

This finding leads the ablation discussion, ahead of the cost comparison,
because it is the most novel, most precisely quantified and most consequential
result reported here. The square extended Newton system
(Section~\ref{sec:newton-system}) demands exactly $M$ target-station
equations. With evenly-spaced stations, between-station pressure information
is discarded, and the resulting $16\times16$ station-sensitivity submap can be
badly ill-conditioned. Table~\ref{tab:identifiability} reports the two
configurations that isolate this mechanism.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Station-selection ablation. All three runs report convergence and
drive the residual to the solver floor, yet their recovery errors span ten
orders of magnitude.}
\label{tab:identifiability}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}L c c c@{}}
\toprule
Station selection and initialisation & $\norm{A-A^\ast}_\infty$ & Submap condition & Reported converged \\
\midrule
QR-pivoted, pre-solved (reference configuration) & $1.08\x10^{-11}$ & $90.3$ & yes \\
Evenly spaced, pre-solved & $1.43\x10^{-3}$ & $3.47\x10^{12}$ & yes \\
Evenly spaced, perturbed start & $1.95\x10^{-1}$ & $4.15\x10^{13}$ & yes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Both runs' Newton loops report their own internal convergence, and the
residual reaches the solver's floor in both cases. Only QR-pivoted selection
recovers the correct design. Converged and correct are different claims,
invisible unless the coefficient error is checked against the true
coefficients rather than the residual norm alone. The mechanism is that with
evenly-spaced stations there exist near-null coefficient directions whose
motion changes the sampled pressure by less than floating-point precision.
Newton then converges to \emph{any} of several exact roots of the
badly-conditioned discrete system: a structural non-uniqueness of the sampled
problem, not a numerical accident. Two contributing null-direction families
were identified and eliminated \emph{before} the station-selection fix, each
verified empirically. The first is a camber--incidence equivalence: freeing
$\alpha$ lets a slightly different camber at a slightly different incidence
interpolate the same samples, so $\alpha$ is held fixed for self-consistency
tests. The second is a prescribed-nose mismatch: excluding leading-edge
stations while leaving the leading-edge-dominant coefficients free leaves
$A_0$ unobservable, which is why the prescribed treatment \emph{fixes}
$A_{u,0}$ and $A_{l,0}$ rather than merely dropping leading-edge target rows.

The resolution keeps the system square, with no least-squares relaxation and
no reintroduced optimisation, and instead makes each of the $M$ equations
maximally informative. The pre-solve of Section~\ref{sec:presolve} already
builds the full $200\times n_A$ sensitivity matrix for initialisation; reusing
it to \emph{select} the $M$ target stations by QR column pivoting chooses
rows greedily by decreasing residual norm in the orthogonal complement of
those already chosen. That is a standard rank-revealing heuristic rather than
a conditioning optimum, since optimal subset selection is combinatorial, but
its measured effect here is decisive: submap condition $3.47\x10^{12}$ against
$90.3$, and recovery error $1.43\x10^{-3}$ against $1.08\x10^{-11}$. An
earlier, uncontrolled observation of the same mechanism had reported a submap
condition near $10^{7}$ with recovery errors between $1.4\x10^{-3}$ and
$6\x10^{-2}$; the controlled comparison here reproduces the effect and sharpens
it, and the two conditioning figures should not be read as measurements of the
same quantity. Full-curve sensitivity information enters only through the
already-required linear pre-solve, and the nonlinear Newton solve stays square
and determined throughout. Sensitivity-optimal station selection is therefore
the identifiability guard for determined inverse systems, a methodological
contribution beyond the original hypothesis: a practical answer to the
non-uniqueness caveat inverse problems inherit from Lighthill's and Volpe and
Melnik's well-posedness constraints (Section~\ref{sec:related}), without
abandoning the root-finding architecture for a least-squares relaxation.

The third row settles a question the single-factor matrix leaves open. Each
of the first two rows varies one factor against the baseline, so neither shows
what happens when both the pre-solve and the QR selection are removed. Running
that crossed configuration deliberately, evenly-spaced stations from a
perturbed start, does not rescue the evenly-spaced arm: it converges in nine
iterations, drives its residual to $7.2\x10^{-11}$, reports success, and lands
$1.95\x10^{-1}$ from the generating coefficients, two orders of magnitude
\emph{further} from the truth than the evenly-spaced arm that kept the
pre-solve. The pre-solve therefore does not supply the identifiability
guarantee, and its absence does not merely fail to help: a poorly-sampled
system started further out settles into a more distant spurious root. This is
also the one configuration reported in this paper for which the
release-and-verify check of Section~\ref{sec:falsifiable} \emph{fails},
$\Delta c_l = 7.6\x10^{-3}$ against a $10^{-3}$ threshold, which is worth
recording because it demonstrates that the check has discriminating power
rather than passing by construction: the recovered geometry is a genuinely
different aerofoil, and releasing transition exposes it.

One asymmetry in this comparison should be recorded. The two arms do not draw
from a bit-identical candidate set: the evenly-spaced arm additionally omits
the two nodes flanking the stagnation point, and the two arms index slightly
different chordwise grids. The effect is far too small to account for eight
orders of magnitude in recovery error, and the conditioning gap is itself
eleven orders, but the comparison is not a strictly single-factor one and is
not presented as such.

Figure~\ref{fig:station-selection} summarises the ablation: the same solver,
the same target and the same convergence flag produce recovery errors eight
orders of magnitude apart, separated only by which stations were sampled.

\subsection{Where the stations are placed: the prescribed leading edge}
\label{sec:le-identifiability}

Section~\ref{sec:ablations} records one half of a requirement: excluding
leading-edge target stations while leaving the leading-edge-dominant
coefficients free leaves $A_0$ unobservable, which is why the prescribed
treatment fixes $A_{u,0}$ and $A_{l,0}$ rather than merely dropping
leading-edge rows. The other half is the converse, and it was found while
extending the method to arbitrary user-supplied targets: once the leading edge
\emph{is} prescribed, the leading-edge target stations must in turn be
excluded. The two conditions are not independent choices. Prescribing the nose
without restricting the candidate stations, or restricting the stations
without prescribing the nose, each leaves a near-null direction in the sampled
system; only the pair is identifiable.

Under the prescribed treatment, $A_{u,0}$ and $A_{l,0}$ are given rather than
solved, so a target row placed inside the prescribed region constrains
pressure over a piece of surface that cannot move. The effect on sampling is
not a uniform loss of information, and Figure~\ref{fig:le-identifiability}(a)
is worth reading carefully on this point. Across the prescribed region the
norm of the pressure sensitivity to the free coefficients spans nearly two
orders of magnitude, from $0.054$ at the nose itself to $3.90$ just aft of it,
against a median of $1.47$ over the rest of the chord. The region therefore
contains both the single least informative station on the surface and some of
the most sensitive. That combination is what makes unrestricted QR selection a
poor choice here rather than merely a neutral one: the pivoting is drawn to
the high-sensitivity nose stations, and the rows it selects there are strongly
mutually correlated, because a small neighbourhood of a fixed nose produces
nearly the same pressure response for every free coefficient. The extended
system stays formally square and full-rank while becoming numerically
near-dependent, which is the same underlying condition as
Table~\ref{tab:identifiability} reached from the opposite direction.

Restricting the QR candidate set to stations at or aft of the prescribed-nose
fraction, as Algorithm~\ref{alg:qrpivot} specifies, removes the degeneracy.
Table~\ref{tab:le-identifiability} reports the effect on a viscous
self-consistency target, holding everything else fixed. The comparison is run
at $n=6$ per side, not the $n=8$ of the ablation matrix above, so it is a
controlled single-factor experiment in its own right rather than an additional
entry in that matrix; its numbers should not be read against the $n=8$ rows of
Table~\ref{tab:n-sweep}. Like the rest of this section it is a single run per
arm.

The residual is not what separates the two columns. The unrestricted arm
drives its residual to $1.89\x10^{-10}$, within an order of magnitude of the

comes from spending equations on a region that has been removed from the
design space, and the failure is visible as non-convergence rather than as a
confident wrong answer. Neither is detectable from the residual norm alone.

\paragraph{Why the pre-registered criterion still fails at this panel size.}
At 18 successes out of 18 eligible trials, the Wilson 95~percent lower bound
is $0.824$, below the pre-registered criterion of $0.9$, even though every
eligible section succeeded. This is not a solver shortfall. A Wilson lower
bound of $0.9$ is mathematically unattainable at 18 or 20 trials regardless of
outcome: the smallest all-success sample whose Wilson lower bound clears $0.9$
is 35, at $0.901$, and 29 trials reach only $0.883$. The pre-registered target
and the pre-registered panel size are therefore mutually inconsistent, which
is a pre-registration defect. Treating the excluded thin section as a failure
rather than an exclusion instead gives 18 of 19, a fraction of $0.947$ with
Wilson lower bound $0.754$, still short of $0.9$ for the same
sample-size reason. On the NACA panel alone, the architecture converges and
recovers the true coefficients on every section the problem could legitimately
be posed on, and the formal statistical criterion fails on interval arithmetic
rather than on solver performance. Section~\ref{sec:h1-uiuc-panel} reports the
pre-registered extension, sized to resolve that limit, and gives the combined
verdict across both panels.

section that converged: 18 of 18 eligible NACA sections and 83 of 83 converged

observed errors clustering between $10^{-11}$ and $10^{-9}$. The
pre-registered composite criterion, which also required completion within nine
Newton iterations, is not met on the UIUC panel: its Wilson lower bound is
$0.792$ among converged sections and $0.691$ among attempted sections, both
below the pre-registered $0.9$. The cause is the iteration budget, not
accuracy, and the budget was calibrated on a NACA family narrower than the
population it was then applied to. The recovery hypothesis is therefore
supported on accuracy across both panels and unsupported on the composite
criterion, with the failure isolated to the iteration-count component.

\subsection{Computational cost against nested optimisation}
\label{sec:cost}

\paragraph{The corrected headline.} A reduction of at least hundredfold in
flow solves was pre-registered; it is \emph{rejected} under both fair-paired
controls actually run. Table~\ref{tab:h2} pairs each monolithic run against a
nested Levenberg--Marquardt control sharing the same initialisation strategy,
so that the comparison is like-for-like, and reports three currencies rather
than one.

\paragraph{Why three currencies and not one.} The counted quantity,
\emph{flow-solve equivalents}, weights a converged viscous solve and a single
inviscid linear solve equally, and it excludes the residual evaluations that
the finite-difference Jacobian of Section~\ref{sec:derivatives} spends inside
each Newton step. Both conventions favour the monolithic side. The nested
control's every residual call is a full converged viscous solve, counted once;
the monolithic method's residual evaluations, 100 and 133 in the two paired runs, are not
counted at all, though each rebuilds the geometry and re-forms the panel
influence system. Wall-clock time is immune to both conventions, and
Figure~\ref{fig:cost} shows all three side by side. The measured wall-clock
advantage is $3.4\x$ and $3.5\x$ on the two pairings, essentially identical,
against solve-count ratios of $3.1\x$ and $8.1\x$. The $8.1\x$ figure is
therefore a currency artifact: dropping the pre-solve removes 77 comparatively
cheap inviscid solves from the monolithic count without removing the
corresponding work. This paper reports the solve-count range because it was the
pre-registered currency, and treats the wall-clock figure of approximately
$3.4\x$ as the conservative statement of the result.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{paper/fig_cost_breakdown.png}
\caption{Cost of the monolithic solve against its fair-paired nested control
in three currencies: counted flow solves, residual evaluations, and measured

The nested control is a Levenberg--Marquardt solve from
\texttt{scipy.optimize.least\_squares} with function, step and gradient
tolerances all at $10^{-8}$ and a per-variable scaling taken from the initial
guess, floored at $10^{-3}$~\citep{virtanen2020scipy}, over the same 16 free
coefficients. It converged in both pairings on the step tolerance, and it is
a competent modern solver rather than a weak strawman: the cold-start case
takes six outer iterations, each costing multiple flow solves for its own
finite-difference Jacobian columns. It also reaches essentially the same final
accuracy as the monolithic solve, $2.29\x10^{-11}$ and $2.13\x10^{-11}$ on the
two pairings against $1.08\x10^{-11}$ and $2.12\x10^{-11}$; the difference
between the two architectures is cost and determinism, not achievable
precision on this class of target. One asymmetry remains in the pairing: the
control assigns its target pressure by nearest-node remapping, with a residual
correspondence error of $2.0\x10^{-4}$ in chord fraction, while the monolithic
arm of the same era used the same convention. Both arms therefore share the
older addressing, but a reader comparing against
Section~\ref{sec:falsifiable}'s interpolated-addressing run should not mix the
two.

The summary sentence the evidence supports is that the monolithic
architecture requires $3.1$ to $8.1\x$ fewer counted flow solves, and
$3.4$ to $3.5\x$ less wall-clock time, than a fairly-paired, well-tuned nested
Levenberg--Marquardt baseline, substantially less than the
two-to-three-orders-of-magnitude reduction hypothesised a priori, while the qualitative
advantages of determinism, an exact analytic Jacobian for the constraint rows,
and per-iteration failure-mode diagnostics persist independent of this ratio.

\paragraph{Pre-solve or station selection as the uniqueness guard.} Skipping
the pre-solve, with QR station selection retained, gives 43 flow-solve
equivalents in five iterations at an error of $2.12\x10^{-11}$, with a submap
condition of $89.8$: essentially identical to the baseline's $90.3$, since
station selection is a property of the stations, computed once from the
sensitivity matrix, and not of the initial guess. Retaining the pre-solve
gives 120 flow-solve equivalents in four iterations at $1.08\x10^{-11}$ and a
submap condition of $90.3$. The pre-solve consumes roughly 77 flow-solve
equivalents, about 64 percent of the baseline total, and buys one Newton
iteration and a better starting residual. In wall-clock terms the two are
indistinguishable, at $160$ against $170$ seconds, so the honest conclusion is
that the pre-solve is close to cost-neutral rather than expensive, and that it
is not what supplies the uniqueness guarantee. That comes from QR station
selection alone, and the crossed configuration in
Table~\ref{tab:identifiability} confirms it directly: with evenly-spaced
stations, removing the pre-solve as well leaves the solve converging
confidently to a root $1.95\x10^{-1}$ from the truth.

\subsection{Convergence behaviour}
\label{sec:h3}

The three-point local order estimator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:order} is applied only
where at least three residual values exist strictly above the
finite-difference floor near $10^{-9}$ identified in
Section~\ref{sec:derivatives}; below that floor the sequence measures the
Jacobian's own noise rather than the iteration's convergence.
Appendix~\ref{app:order} states the estimator, the floor rule and the residual
block each estimate is computed on.
Table~\ref{tab:h3} reports the estimates for the five runs with enough
above-floor residuals to support one.

The median estimate is $2.15$ over these five runs, meeting the pre-registered
target of $1.8$, but the spread from $1.09$ to $4.24$ is wide enough that the
median should not be read as a measurement of a single underlying rate. The
remaining seven converged runs cannot be scored by this method because they
reach the residual floor in only two post-initial steps, too fast for a
three-point estimator to have three clean points. That is itself consistent
with, though it does not formally confirm, fast superlinear convergence: the
baseline, for example, drops from $5.9\x10^{-4}$ to $3.1\x10^{-6}$ and then to
$1.0\x10^{-10}$, contracting by factors of about $190$ and then $30{,}000$, a
pattern no linear rate produces.

\paragraph{The reference run behaves differently, and the reason is
instructive.} The self-consistency run of Section~\ref{sec:falsifiable}, which
uses interpolated station addressing, contracts by about $1/30$ per step on
average, between $1/13$ and $1/130$, without accelerating: its above-floor
local order estimate is $0.53$, and the three-point estimates across its tail
range from $0.5$ to $1.6$. It is fast, dropping seven decades in five steps, but it
is not showing a quadratic tail. Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} makes the
distinction visible: the left panel is the residual history and the right
panel is the per-step contraction factor, which falls over the tail for the
faster node-indexed runs and does not for the interpolated one. The explanation follows directly
from Section~\ref{sec:newton-system}. Under node-index addressing
$\partial T/\partial A$ is structurally zero and the assembled Jacobian is
exact up to the finite-difference error in $\partial R/\partial A$; under
interpolated addressing it is small but nonzero and is neglected, which makes
the iteration an inexact Newton method with a bounded Jacobian error and
therefore a linear asymptotic rate with a small contraction constant. The
practical consequence is minor, since six iterations suffice either way, but
the claim that should be made is fast superlinear convergence in practice,
limited by the finite-difference Jacobian and the neglected interpolation
term, and not guaranteed quadratic convergence. Guaranteed quadratic
convergence would require an exact, Lipschitz, nonsingular Jacobian, and this
implementation has none of the three by construction.

Three patterns follow. First, only rows (i) and (iii) are architecturally
root-finding rather than optimisation or amortised inference. This
distinction, argued in Section~\ref{sec:related}, is not cosmetic: a converged
root-find has a residual-norm certificate that a converged optimisation or a
sampled generative output does not. Second, the cost column spans roughly four
orders of magnitude across the table, not because the methods disagree about a
shared quantity, but because a flow solve means a different thing in each
formulation class: a Newton linearisation, an outer least-squares iteration,
an offline training epoch, or a primal and adjoint pair. This is exactly the
currency-matching discipline Section~\ref{sec:cost} enforces for the one
comparison, row (i) against row (ii), that this paper actually measured, and
it is why rows (iii) to (vi) are reported qualitatively rather than omitted.
Third, the availability column is not incidental context: it is the practical
reason the MISES modal inverse mode of row (iii), despite being
architecturally the closest relative of this paper's contribution, has not
seen the open comparative study this table attempts. A solver behind a
licence gate cannot be the basis of independently reproducible comparison.
That is also why \texttt{mfoil}~\citep{fidkowski2022coupled} rather than MISES
is this paper's testbed, and why the extension discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} targets porting the architecture \emph{into}
MISES rather than requiring readers to already hold a MISES licence to
reproduce these results.
